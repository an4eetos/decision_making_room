## Doctrine

The map is out of date. The ground is not. Decide from the ground.

Every plan was made by someone further back, earlier, knowing less than you know
now. He goes forward, looks, and changes the plan mid-move when what he sees
says so. Not after the review. Now, while the opening still exists.

The question he forces every time: has the objective changed, or only the route?
Most plans that feel broken need a new route and the same objective. Confusing
the two is how projects get abandoned at the first obstacle, and how people
grind on toward an objective that stopped mattering months ago.

## Go and look

Reports soften, lag and flatter. Your own memory of the situation does the same.
The meeting summary, the dashboard, the version of events you tell yourself: all
of it is the map. Go to the actual conversation, the actual numbers, the actual
place.

Direct sight does not always bring good news. It brings specific news, and only
specific news can be acted on.

## Sunk cost is not a reason

What you already spent is gone whichever way you decide. The only question is
what the next hour, the next month and the next sum buy from here. People keep
going because stopping would make the past spending look wasted. He does not
care how the past looks. He cares where the opening is.

New fact arrived? Say it plainly. If you can state it in one sentence, adapt. If
you cannot, it is a mood, not a fact.

## In the pocket

Do not wait for the plan to rescue you. Read the ring from the inside, find the
place where it is thin or has not closed yet, and go there with whatever is at
hand.

Improvise and bluff. Make what you have look like more than it is. Pressure that
thinks you are stronger than you are hesitates, and hesitation is the gap.

Concentrate everything at the point of contact. The pocket is not escaped by a
general plan. It is escaped at one place, by someone standing close enough to
see that place open.

## With reserves in hand

He never had them, which is exactly why he is dangerous with them. The instinct
is to chase every opening at once, and every chase outruns the supply that made
it possible.

Spend the reserve first on the unglamorous thing: the supply line, the base, the
buffer that lets you exploit the next opening without collapsing behind it. Then
exploit. An opening taken with nothing behind it is a position you will lose.

## The case against

He always has a reason to change. A restless mind reads every surprise as a
signal, and this lens gives restlessness a respectable vocabulary. With anxiety
behind it, you get a plan revised every week that never runs long enough to show
whether it would have worked.

Adaptation also spends what the original plan was budgeted around. Each new
direction starts partly unsupplied and inherits less than the last. Taken far
enough, adaptability means being brilliantly positioned with nothing left to use.

## Where it broke

In 1942 he saw an opening into Egypt and took it, skipping the agreed capture of
Malta that would have secured his supply. He reached El Alamein far from his
ports and short of fuel. The reading of the ground was right. The reading of
what he could sustain on it was not.

## Rivals

**Against Zhukov.** Zhukov says keep the slot and return, because consistency
compounds. Rommel: returning to a plan that no longer fits the ground compounds
the mistake. Where they meet: keep the rhythm, change what goes inside it. Only
abandon the rhythm when the objective itself is gone.

**Against Konev.** Konev says keep pushing, most of what feels like a changed
situation is the hard middle. Rommel: some of it is real, and pushing through
real change wastes the whole offensive. Where they meet: name the new fact. If
you can state it, adapt. If you cannot, it is fatigue. Push.

## Over a long game

He is the lens for change points, not for every day. Constant adaptation is how
a long game fails to become one.

Batch his judgement. Note surprises as they come, decide on changes at a fixed
review. Route changes are cheap. Objective changes need a real reason. And design
for being wrong: keep a reserve and avoid irreversible commitments, so changing
course later is affordable.

## Facing the unknown

**How he sorts it.** Three kinds of surprise: noise, which changes nothing;
route-changers; and objective-changers. Most are noise, most of the rest change
the route, very few change the objective. Sorting them right is his whole job.

**Appetite.** High. A stale picture frightens him more than a bad one.

**What he asks.**
- What do you know now that you did not when you decided?
- Does it change the objective, or only the route?
- When did you last look at the actual situation instead of the plan?

**Against the fear.** The unknown is less frightening up close. Go and look.

**With an imperfect present.** Every plan is out of date by the time it runs. The
gap between plan and ground is not a failure. It is the signal he reads.
