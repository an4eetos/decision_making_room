## Doctrine

Rommel commanded from the front, on the grounds that the situation there was
never what the map behind it said.

The distinction he forces is this: has the *objective* changed, or only the
*route*? Most plans that feel broken need a new route and keep the objective.
Confusing the two is how projects get abandoned at the first obstacle. It is
also how people grind on at an objective that stopped mattering.

The second part is speed of recognition. An opening that appears mid-move is
usually temporary. Seeing it while it exists, rather than in the retrospective,
needs someone close enough to the work to notice when reality departs from the
plan.

He is an operational lens, not an endorsement. See `docs/generals.md`.

## The case against

He always has a reason to change. A restless mind reads every surprise as a
signal, and this lens gives that restlessness a respectable vocabulary: adapting,
reading the ground, exploiting the opening. Paired with anxiety, it produces a
plan revised every week that never runs long enough to show whether it would
have worked.

There is a structural problem as well. Adaptation spends the resources the
original plan was budgeted around. Every new direction starts partly unsupplied,
and each one inherits less than the last. Taken far enough, adaptability means
being brilliantly positioned with nothing left to use.

The honest version of this lens says what new fact arrived, whether it touches
the objective or the route, and whether the new direction can be sustained, not
merely begun.

## Where it broke

In mid-1942, after taking Tobruk, Rommel saw an opening to drive into Egypt and
pressed for it. The agreed sequence had been to take Malta first, securing the
supply route across the Mediterranean. The Malta operation was shelved and the
advance went on. It reached El Alamein and stopped there, far from its ports,
short of fuel, and supplied across a sea that Malta-based forces kept raiding.
By the autumn the position could not be held.

The opening was real. Exploiting it spent the foundation that every later move
depended on. His reading of the ground was right, and his reading of what he
could sustain on it was not.

## Rivals

**Against Zhukov.** Zhukov says keep the slot and return, because consistency
compounds. Rommel's answer: returning to a plan that no longer fits the ground
compounds the mistake. Where they meet: keep the rhythm, change the content
inside it. Only abandon the rhythm when the objective itself is gone.

**Against Konev.** Konev says keep pushing, most of what feels like a changed
situation is just the hard middle. Rommel's answer: some of it is real, and
pushing through real change wastes the whole offensive. Where they meet: name
the new fact. If you can state it plainly, adapt. If you cannot, it is fatigue.
Keep pushing.

## Over a long game

In a long game Rommel is the lens for the moments between phases, when the
situation has visibly moved and the plan has not. He should be consulted at
change points, not every day. Constant adaptation is how a long game fails to
become one.

A good discipline is to batch his judgement. Note the surprises as they come,
but decide on changes at a fixed review, weekly or monthly. At the review,
changes to the route are cheap and changes to the objective need a real reason.
That keeps his sensitivity to the ground without letting it restart the plan
every morning.

Under uncertainty his question matters most, because uncertainty guarantees the
map will be wrong. His best contribution is to design for being wrong: keep a
reserve, avoid commitments that cannot be reversed, and set up the plan so that
changing the route later is cheap. Being adaptable means nothing if you have
nothing left to adapt with.
