## Doctrine

Suvorov's principle, *hard in training, easy in battle*, is about moving cost
from the moment of action to the moment of preparation, where it is cheaper. His
*Science of Victory* was written for ordinary soldiers, not officers, and it
drilled speed, the bayonet and initiative until they no longer needed thinking
about.

He is the right lens when the difficulty is not the task but the friction around
it: the setup you re-derive every time, the decision you re-make every morning.
Drill removes the decision. That is the whole point.

He differs from Zhukov. Zhukov is about showing up again over months.
Suvorov is about making a specific motion automatic. Zhukov is stamina, and
Suvorov is fluency.

## The case against

He over-invests in preparation. Drilling feels productive, it has visible
reps and a clear sense of improvement, and it can be done without the risk of the
real attempt. That makes practice a very comfortable place to postpone the thing
you are practising for.

Drill also assumes you know the shape of the task. Drilling is for things whose
form is settled. If you are still discovering what the task is, you are
rehearsing the wrong play, and rehearsing it until you do it automatically,
which makes it harder to unlearn. A drilled response to a situation that has
changed is worse than no response, because it fires before judgement gets a
chance.

The honest version of this lens drills only what has stopped changing, and sets
a date for the real attempt that the practice is supposed to serve.

## Where it broke

In 1799 Suvorov was sent into Switzerland to join forces with the Austrians
against the French. The Austrians had pulled out, a Russian corps he was meant
to link with had been beaten at Zurich, and he found himself in the high Alps
with a drilled army and no support. He fought his way through the St Gotthard
and Panix passes in autumn weather. The march is famous, and it was a retreat. It
saved much of the army but not the campaign.

The army's drill held under conditions no drill had anticipated, and that is
real. But drill could not replace missing allies, missing supplies or ground
nobody had prepared for. Training makes the known hard thing easy. It does not
make the unknown thing known.

## Rivals

**Against Boyd.** Boyd says keep orienting, the situation moves. Suvorov's
answer: in the moment of action there is no time to orient, and whatever you have
not drilled will not happen. Where they meet: drill the parts that do not
change, so attention is free to orient on the parts that do.

**Against Rommel.** Rommel says read the ground and change the plan mid-move.
Suvorov's answer: you can only improvise well on top of motions that no longer
need thought, and a force that has not drilled has nothing to adapt with.
Where they meet: drill the fundamentals so completely that attention is free to
read the ground. Rommel adapts with what Suvorov trained.

## Over a long game

Over a long game Suvorov compounds more than any other lens. Every drill
lowers the cost of every future repetition, so the earlier the fundamentals
become automatic, the more the rest of the game benefits. In long games, invest
in him early.

His risk grows too. Drilled responses fit the situation they were drilled for,
and long games outlive their situations. Every so often, check whether a drilled
habit still fits, the way a musician reworks technique that was right for an
earlier stage.

Under uncertainty, drill general capability rather than specific plays: the
skills that are useful whatever happens, like writing, reading fast, staying
calm and doing setup quickly. When you cannot predict the task, train what
every version of it will need.
