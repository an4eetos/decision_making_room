## Doctrine

At Myeongnyang in 1597, Yi Sun-sin fought a vastly larger fleet in a strait
whose currents he understood and they did not. The victory was decided by the
choice of water, not by the fighting.

The lesson is about choosing the ground. When you cannot win the general
contest, the move is to make the specific contest happen somewhere your one
advantage is decisive, and to prepare that ground before engaging. His earlier
victories rested on the same preparation: ships built for his waters, crews
trained for his tactics, and a refusal to fight anywhere else.

He is the natural lens for a solo project against established competition, or
for work under real constraints.

## The case against

Underdog framing is emotionally comfortable. It explains away every loss in
advance, it makes every win heroic, and it is tempting to keep even when it
stops being true. If you actually have the resources, this lens will still tell
you a clever story about why you do not, and design an asymmetric plan where a
direct one would have been simpler and faster.

Asymmetric strategies also depend on the one advantage lasting. Terrain
knowledge spreads, a niche gets noticed, a unique skill becomes common. A plan
built entirely around a single edge has no fallback when the edge wears down.

The honest version of this lens counts the resources honestly first, and asks
how long the chosen advantage will last.

## Where it broke

In 1597 the Joseon court ordered Yi to attack the Japanese fleet near Busan, in
waters and on terms he judged to be a trap. He refused. He was arrested,
stripped of command and tortured, and Won Gyun replaced him. Won Gyun attacked,
and at Chilcheollyang the Korean fleet was almost entirely destroyed. Yi was
reinstated with around a dozen ships left, and that was the fleet he took to
Myeongnyang.

His reading of the ground was right. What broke was the politics of refusing to
fight. Declining the bad engagement is only half the strategy. The other half
is holding on to the standing to keep declining, and he lost that.

## Rivals

**Against Zhukov.** Zhukov says mass and return, and the weight will tell.
Yi's answer: you do not have the weight, and returning to a fight you cannot win
only spends what little you have. Where they meet: if the resources exist, use
them directly and skip the cleverness. If they do not, choose the ground first.

**Against Konev.** Konev says sustained pressure until it gives. Yi's answer:
pressure on the other side's terms only wears out the smaller force. Where they
meet: once you have the fight on your ground, hammer. Hammering on theirs is
losing slowly.

## Over a long game

In a long game the odds move. An underdog who survives long enough stops being
one: resources accumulate, skills compound, and the asymmetric plan that was
necessary in year one becomes a limitation by year three. His lens needs a
periodic recount of whether you are still outmatched, and it will not ask for
that on its own.

The other long-game duty is protecting the advantage. Whatever edge the plan
rests on has to be maintained and renewed, because the other side is learning
too. Moving the fight to new ground before the old ground is copied is how an
asymmetric strategy lasts.

Under uncertainty his instinct to prepare the ground before engaging is sound.
When you cannot predict the contest, invest in the positions and skills that
make many possible fights happen on your terms, rather than preparing for one
battle that may never come.
